The short version

On cost and simplicity I lean towards a gas combi boiler. A combi heats water as it is used, so there is no hot water cylinder and no tanks in the loft, and the landing cupboard becomes storage.

The weak point is the shower. A combi shower is hot and steady but gentle. It will feel like a step down from the power shower. How much of a step depends on the mains water supply, which nobody has measured yet.

My estimate is about £3,500 to £6,000 for a straightforward combi job, including a new shower, and up to about £9,000 if it turns out to be complicated. The work takes about two days, a little longer if the boiler has to move.

Two checks decide most of it: the bucket test on the mains water, and whether the gas pipe to the boiler is big enough.

The shower: what to expect

The present shower will almost certainly have to be replaced, whichever option is chosen. A power shower has a pump designed to draw from a loft tank. Triton, one of the main makers, says its power showers must be fed from a tank and cannot be connected to mains pressure. Once the tanks are gone there is nothing for the pump to draw from, and a pump must not be fitted to a combi's supply either.

How a new shower will feel compared with now

A power shower boosts the flow with a pump, so it is probably more forceful than anything fed straight from the mains. Moving to mains water means a different shower, however it is done.

  • Combi. Hot, steady and never runs out, but gentle. A combi can only heat water so fast: about 12 litres a minute from a 30 kW boiler, about 14 from a 35 kW one, and under 10 from a 24 kW one. If you have used a combi shower that was hot but weak, and washing hair took longer, that is the usual reason. It is a flow limit, not a pressure fault.
  • Mains-pressure cylinder. It has no heating-speed limit, because the hot water is already stored. Shower strength then depends on the mains. If the mains delivers 20 litres a minute or more, the shower can come close to what you have now. If the mains delivers only 12 to 14, the shower will be no stronger than a combi, and you would have given up the cupboard for nothing.

So the water test matters more than anything else in this note. When installers visit, ask each one what shower flow, in litres a minute, they expect from your measured mains under each option.

The usual replacement is a thermostatic mixer shower. It needs no pump because the mains provides the pressure, and it holds its temperature steady even when someone runs a tap elsewhere. Fitting one costs roughly £400 to £900 where the tiling and tray can stay, and £800 to £2,000 or more where they have to be redone. A full bathroom refit is typically £3,000 to £8,000 and takes weeks, and it would not make the heating work any sooner. If the bathroom is part of the renovation, a simple shower could go in now and the rest could follow.

The three options

A combi boiler

One wall-hung gas boiler does everything. It heats the radiators, and when a hot tap is opened it heats mains water on its way through. The cylinder, both loft tanks, the pump and the valve are all removed, and the airing cupboard is empty afterwards.

Boiler sizes are given in kW, which is the boiler's power. A bigger number means more hot water at once. The flow figures above are the makers' figures for heating the water by 35 degrees, roughly from a cold winter mains to a hot shower. The limit is that the whole house shares that flow. If someone runs a hot tap while another person is in the shower, the shower weakens until the tap is off. A thermostatic shower keeps the temperature steady while that happens. Baths fill more slowly than they would from a cylinder. If the boiler breaks down there is no heating and no hot water until it is repaired.

A boiler with a mains-pressure cylinder

A new gas boiler heats the radiators and a new hot water cylinder in the landing cupboard. Unlike the present cylinder, the new one is fed directly from the mains, so it needs no loft tank and gives mains pressure at every hot tap. Plumbers call this an unvented cylinder, and you will see that word on quotes.

It suits houses where several people want hot water at once, because it has 180 to 210 litres stored and ready. Makers' sizing guides suggest about 210 litres for four people. A cylinder that size is about 1.5 metres tall and 58 cm across, and weighs up to about 250 kg when full, so it is a good deal bigger than the present one. Once the stored water is used, it takes roughly 20 to 35 minutes to reheat.

It has an electric immersion heater, so there is still hot water if the boiler fails. The heating would still be off. The immersion heater costs about three times as much to run as gas, so it is a backup and nothing more. The stored water loses a little heat all day, roughly £50 a year on the makers' figures. The cylinder also has safety valves that need checking periodically, and some makers make a yearly check a condition of the guarantee.

A heat pump with a cylinder

An electric unit outside the house takes heat from the air and uses it to warm the radiators and a hot water cylinder. It replaces the gas boiler entirely. Heat pumps run the radiators cooler than a boiler does, so some radiators usually have to be replaced with larger ones. One survey of installers found this was needed in about seven homes in ten, with six radiators changed on average.

It needs a cylinder, usually a larger one than a gas boiler would use, so the airing cupboard stays in use. Most of the hot water is heated by the heat pump itself. Some systems also use an immersion heater for a weekly high-temperature cycle that guards against bacteria in the stored water. Makers of the newer models say theirs reach that temperature without one, though some owners report otherwise. Any installer should say in writing whether their design relies on the immersion heater and how often.

There is a government grant of £7,500, called the Boiler Upgrade Scheme, and no VAT on heat pump installation until 31 March 2027. The grant rules are under the rules and guarantees.

Which way I lean, and why

What decides between a combi and a cylinder is how much hot water is wanted at the same moment, not how many people live in the house. In this house showers are rarely taken one after another, and hot water use is spread through the day. A combi handles that comfortably, and a cylinder would spend most of its time holding hot water nobody is using. That is why I lean towards the combi.

The cylinder would give you four things a combi does not: a stronger shower if the mains is good, a shower that holds up when a tap is run, a faster bath, and hot water if the boiler breaks down. For those you would pay roughly £1,000 to £2,500 more to install it, about £50 a year in lost heat, and most of the airing cupboard. Whether that is good value depends mostly on the first one. If a forceful shower matters a lot to the household, that is a fair reason to choose the cylinder, provided the mains test says it would deliver.

A cylinder would not make the heating any more dependable. Reliable heating is what matters most, and with either gas option it depends on the boiler. The protection is the same for both: a boiler with a long guarantee, serviced every year, and a couple of electric heaters in reserve.

Is one easier to fit?

Neither is simple, and each has one awkward job. A combi burns gas faster than the present boiler while it is heating water, so the gas pipe from the meter will probably need replacing with a larger one. It also needs a hot water pipe run from the boiler to join the existing hot pipes, which at the moment all start at the airing cupboard. The cylinder option avoids both of those but needs a new, larger cold water pipe up to the landing and a safety pipe from the cylinder to a drain outside. The combi takes more equipment out of the house and puts less back. With the renovation opening up floors and walls anyway, the pipe runs should be easier for either.

And the heat pump?

I put it third, for two reasons. It needs a cylinder, so the cupboard is not freed. And it takes longest to arrange: a survey, a design, a grant application and probably new radiators. The only published figure I found for the wait, from one national installer, is 4 to 16 weeks. I do not know what a local firm could manage.

It does have two things going for it. The renovation makes it much easier, because changing radiators is far less disruptive when rooms are already being worked on. And a gas boiler fitted now should last fifteen years or so, which means that changing to a heat pump in a few years would be paying twice. If a heat pump appeals at all, now is the time to find out. One survey and a firm date, asked for alongside the gas quotes, would settle the timing.

The water supply test

With the loft tanks gone, everything depends on what the mains delivers. Three terms come up:

  • Flow is how much water arrives, in litres a minute.
  • Standing pressure is the pressure in the pipe when every tap is shut. It usually looks healthy and says little.
  • Working pressure is the pressure while water is running. This is the figure that matters, and it is always lower than standing pressure.

Pressure is measured in bar. Water companies are expected to supply about 1 bar at the edge of the property with about 9 litres a minute flowing, which is less than a good shower wants.

How to test it

Turn the kitchen cold tap fully on and time how long it takes to fill a 10 litre bucket. Thirty seconds means 20 litres a minute. A full minute means 10. Some taps restrict the flow, so an outside tap gives a truer figure if there is one. Do it between 7 and 9 in the morning, when the street is using most water, and again in the middle of the day.

For pressure, a gauge from a plumbing merchant screws onto an outside tap or a washing machine connection. Read it with all taps shut, then again with the kitchen cold tap running. Write both down.

Cylinder makers quote higher figures. Megaflo asks for 20 litres a minute at 1.5 bar. Vaillant asks for 2 bar and 20 to 25 litres a minute, and says its cylinder still works below that with reduced flow, but should not be fitted at all below 1 bar. Those are what each maker wants for its own product to perform at its best. They are not a pass mark for every cylinder. A combi's requirement depends on the model, and the figures I found ranged from under 1 bar to 1.5 bar, so the installer should go by the manual for the boiler they propose and measure the supply themselves.

The radiators and the sealed system

At present the small loft tank keeps the radiators full and the system runs at low pressure. With that tank gone the heating becomes what is called a sealed system. It is filled from the mains through a valve, and a small pressure vessel takes up the expansion as the water heats. It runs at two to three times the pressure the radiators are used to.

Most systems take this without trouble. Where there is a weak point it tends to show: a weeping radiator valve, a tired joint, occasionally a pinhole in an old radiator. A careful installer pressure-tests the system before fitting the new boiler, so problems turn up at the start. The allowance for surprises in my estimates is meant to cover a valve or two. A radiator that has to be replaced costs about £150 to £450, fitted, and several of those would take the job above my straightforward figure.

The building regulations say the system should be cleaned and flushed when a new boiler goes in, and treated with a chemical that stops corrosion. Boiler makers make this a condition of their guarantees, and most also want a magnetic filter fitted, which catches sludge before it reaches the boiler. There are two ways to clean a system: a chemical flush, or a more forceful power flush, which pumps water through at speed. Opinions differ on which suits an older system, so ask each installer which they intend and why.

The installer needs to reach every radiator. Since the renovation will involve the radiators anyway, any that are due to be changed could be done as part of that. The boiler should not wait for it.

The difference between the combi and the cylinder is the least certain figure here. The simplest way to pin it down is to ask each installer to price both.

Running costs

Under the current price cap, gas costs about 8p a unit and electricity about 26p. A combi and a boiler with a cylinder cost about the same to run, apart from the £50 or so a year the cylinder loses as heat.

A heat pump uses expensive electricity but makes about three units of heat from each unit it uses. Whether it beats gas depends on how well it performs once installed. For a typical house, which is not the same as your own bill, gas heating comes to roughly £1,190 a year. A heat pump would cost about £1,080 if it performs modestly and about £760 if it performs very well. A government-funded trial measured the modest figure on average. Systems fitted by specialists and monitored by their owners average closer to the better one. So the saving is somewhere between nothing and about £300 a year, and it should not be assumed. If gas were removed from the house altogether, the gas standing charge of about £108 a year would be saved as well.

The rules and guarantees

Required by law

  • Anyone working on the gas must be on the Gas Safe register. The engineer carries a photo card, and the back of it lists what they are qualified to work on.
  • A new boiler must be reported to the council's building control. Swindon Borough Council is the authority here. A registered installer does this for you, and a Building Regulations certificate should arrive in the post within 30 days.
  • A mains-pressure cylinder needs an installer with a separate qualification for unvented hot water, often called G3. Being Gas Safe registered is not enough by itself. The cylinder must have a visible safety outlet and a pipe to carry any discharge safely outside. This applies to the cylinder and heat pump options only.

Expected by the building regulations guidance

  • A room thermostat and timer with any new boiler.
  • With a combi, one extra energy-saving measure. A smart thermostat or a control that adjusts to the weather outside both count.
  • The system cleaned, flushed and treated when the boiler is changed.

Conditions of the guarantees

Boiler guarantees run from about 5 to 10 years or more, depending on the make and on who fits it. The longest ones are only available through installers accredited by the maker. Worcester Bosch, for example, asks for the boiler to be registered within 30 days, the system to be flushed, one of its own filters to be fitted, and a service every year. Ask each firm for the length of the guarantee and what you must do to keep it valid, and get it in writing with the quote.

For a heat pump only

  • The grant is £7,500. The installer applies for it and takes it off the bill. The voucher lasts three months. Since April 2026 no energy certificate for the house has been needed.
  • The heat pump must replace the gas boiler completely. A heat pump working alongside a gas boiler does not qualify.
  • Planning permission is not normally needed. The rules are stricter inside a conservation area. There is a conservation area in the village, and the council can say whether our road is inside it.
  • The installer must tell the electricity network, and may need its approval first if the supply to the house is limited.

Questions that could change the choice

These are for the household to answer. Any of them could tip the decision.

  • How much does a forceful shower matter, compared with getting the landing cupboard back?
  • Will the renovation add a second shower, downstairs for instance? Two showers that might run at the same time is the strongest reason to choose a cylinder.
  • Does anyone take baths often enough for a slower fill to be a nuisance?
  • How much would it matter if the shower weakened for a moment when someone ran a hot tap?
  • Would you pay the extra for a cylinder to have hot water during a boiler breakdown?
  • Is the boiler staying where it is, or will the renovation move the kitchen?
  • Does anything else in the house use gas, such as a hob or a fire?
  • If a heat pump were chosen, how long could you manage with temporary heating?

What to ask each installer

  1. Do you cover our area, and when could you start?

Insurance

I would not count on the insurance paying towards the replacement. I looked at the home emergency leaflet Allianz publishes for 2026. Your own policy may be worded differently, so please check it.

  • It pays for emergency repairs up to £1,000 for each emergency.
  • It specifically excludes the cost of replacing cylinders, water tanks, showers and radiators.
  • It offers £500 towards a new boiler, but only where the boiler itself cannot be economically repaired and has been serviced within the last three years. Here the fault is at the cylinder or its pipework, so I doubt this applies.
  • It allows up to £250 for somewhere else to stay if the house is unfit to live in.

It would be sensible to tell Allianz what is planned before ordering anything.

What I would do next

  1. Do the bucket test, and find the gas meter.
  2. Talk through the questions above, the shower and the cupboard especially.
  3. Ring Archers, James Rayner and BSK for a visit and an itemised quote for a combi, with the cylinder version priced alongside and the expected shower flow stated for each.
  4. If a heat pump is of any interest, ask one firm for a survey and their earliest date at the same time.
  5. Check each firm's Gas Safe number online.
  6. Compare the quotes against the questions above and the prices in this note.
